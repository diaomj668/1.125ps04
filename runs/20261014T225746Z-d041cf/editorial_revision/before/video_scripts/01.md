# New ways to supervise, inspect, and constrain coding agents

As-of: 2026-10-14

**Presentation direction:** Planned for synthetic narration. Retain the spoken AI disclosure. Use simple diagrams and clearly labeled illustrative screens, not fabricated product demonstrations. Evidence annotations are not spoken.

**Visual direction:** Title card, date, and three columns: “Supervision,” “Secret checking,” and “Diagnosis.”

**Narration:**
This presentation uses AI-generated narration. As of October 14, 2026, some of the most concrete coding-assistant developments concern how you supervise an agent and inspect its work—not demonstrated improvements in coding quality. For someone building a small app or script, that distinction matters. Access lets you direct work. Checks flag possible problems. Diagnostic artifacts help you investigate. None of these, on its own, establishes that your project is finished or safe.

**Visual direction:** Diagram connecting a phone to an agent running on a computer. Keep the label “Computer must stay on and online” visible.

**Narration:**
First, supervision. Cursor announced that its iOS app can display local agents running on your computer and accept replies. The important qualification is where execution happens: the agent stays on your computer. Remote control does not move that work elsewhere, and your computer must remain on and online. This is a change in access to an existing agent, not evidence that the agent writes better code. [01-c1]

**Narration:**
GitHub also launched ReviewBench, an available evaluation resource for code-review agents. Its reported corpus contains 219 pull requests from 187 public repositories across 19 languages. That provides something to evaluate against; its availability does not establish that a particular reviewer suits your small project. Large-repository workloads need not resemble a small script or single-file change. [01-c4]
